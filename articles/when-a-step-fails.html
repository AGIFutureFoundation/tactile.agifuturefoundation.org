Introduction: the night the line stopped

Picture a production line at two in the morning. The line has run ten thousand units without a stop. Then a sensor goes dark, a conveyor halts, and the whole line stands still while the night shift gathers around a panel of blinking lights. The question on everyone's mind is not a technical one, at first. It is a human one: what happened? And the second question, asked thirty seconds later, is harder: is it broken, or is it waiting? The difference between those two answers is the difference between a five-minute restart and a four-hour rebuild, and getting it wrong in either direction is expensive. Restart a line that is genuinely broken and you break it further. Rebuild a line that was only waiting and you have thrown away four hours for nothing.

That scene is a generic one — no real factory, no real night shift, no real sensor — but the shape of it is the shape of every workflow failure. A step stops. The record says it stopped. The record does not say why, and it does not say whether the step will finish if you wait. Everything in this article is about closing that gap: turning "a step failed" from a dead end into a diagnosis, a decision, and, eventually, a lesson.

1. First, name the failure

The most common mistake in failure handling is also the most understandable: treating all failures as the same thing. A step reports an error, and the response is a reflex — try again, or escalate, or stop — applied without asking what kind of error it was. The reflex is sometimes right, which is why it survives. It is also sometimes catastrophically wrong, which is why the discipline begins with a different move: before you do anything, name the failure.

A useful taxonomy has five kinds, and the five cover most of what a workflow will ever meet.

Transient failures are the weather of distributed systems: a network partition, a momentary overload, a service that is briefly unreachable. Nothing is wrong with the step, the inputs, or the plan. The world hiccuped. Transient failures are the ones retries exist for, and they are the only kind where "try again" is the right first move without further thought. A failure is transient only if there is evidence that the cause has passed or will pass. "It might be transient" is not a diagnosis. It is a hope, and hope is not a failure-handling policy.

Permanent failures are the opposite: the step cannot succeed as written, and trying again will produce the same result. The input is malformed. The account does not exist. The file the step needs was never created. Permanent failures are the ones where retry is pure waste — worse than waste, because each retry consumes budget, time, and attention, and can trigger side effects. The correct response to a permanent failure is not a retry. It is a stop, a report, and a human decision about whether the plan itself needs to change.

Business-rule failures sit between the two and are the easiest to misread. The step ran correctly and the answer is no: the credit check failed, the sample did not meet the threshold, the order exceeds the limit. Nothing is broken. The workflow did its job and the job's answer was a refusal. Treating a business-rule failure as a technical failure — retrying a credit check three times in the hope that the applicant becomes creditworthy — is a category error. The plan should say, in advance, which outcomes are refusals and which are errors, and the failure handling should treat them differently.

Timeouts are the most ambiguous kind, and they get a section of their own below, because a timeout is not a fact about the step. It is a fact about the observer: the step did not finish within the time allowed. Whether the step was slow, stuck, or dead is a separate question, and the timeout does not answer it.

Missing-input failures are the quietest kind and often the most damaging. The step did not fail. It never started, because something it needed was not there — an upstream output that was never produced, a file that was never delivered, a signal that never arrived. The workflow's state says "waiting" while the truth is "abandoned." A step that has waited past its deadline is a failure, even if no error was ever raised. Waiting without a deadline is not patience. It is a failure that nobody has declared yet.

The taxonomy matters because each kind has a different correct response, and the response must be chosen before the failure happens, not improvised during it. A plan that says "on failure, retry three times" has made one decision for five different problems, and four of them will be wrong.

2. Dead or just slow? Timeouts and heartbeats

Of the five kinds, the timeout is the one that causes the most damage, because it is the one that looks like an answer and is not. A timeout says: this step did not finish within the time allowed. It does not say whether the step was about to finish, whether it was stuck halfway, or whether it died an hour ago and nobody noticed. Acting on a timeout as if it were a diagnosis is how workflows double-charge customers, double-ship orders, and double-apply treatments — the step that was killed for being slow had, in fact, already completed its work, and the retry completed it again.

The field's best-known orchestration tools treat this problem with a seriousness that is worth borrowing. AWS Step Functions, Amazon's workflow orchestration service, names its timeout errors explicitly: States.Timeout is reported when a task runs longer than its TimeoutSeconds value, and States.HeartbeatTimeout when a task fails to send a heartbeat for longer than its HeartbeatSeconds value. The distinction is the point. A timeout is a clock running out. A heartbeat is a signal that the step is alive. A step that sends heartbeats is slow. A step that stops sending them is dead — or at least unreachable — and the two cases deserve different responses.

The practical rule that follows is simple to state and easy to violate: never kill a step on the basis of a clock alone. A step should be given a deadline, a heartbeat interval, and a rule for what happens when the heartbeat stops. The rule for a stopped heartbeat is escalation — a human looks, or a monitor report is commissioned, or the step is checked with the cheapest instrument available. Tactile's step_check job, at $1 with a 5-minute turnaround, is the catalogue's version of that rule: a quick check of one step, whether it is complete, in progress, or blocked, from the workflow state you provide.

3. The circuit breaker: stop hammering a failing dependency

There is a failure mode worse than a step failing, and it is a step failing repeatedly, on schedule, against a dependency that is already down. Every retry consumes resources on both sides: the caller's threads, the network, and the struggling service's remaining capacity. Enough retries, from enough callers, will finish what the original failure started — taking down a service that might have recovered if it had been left alone for a minute. The pattern that prevents this has a name borrowed from electrical engineering, and it is one of the most widely used ideas in distributed systems.

The circuit breaker, as described by Martin Fowler — who credits Michael Nygard's book Release It! with popularising it — wraps a call to a remote service in an object that monitors for failures. Once the failures reach a threshold, the breaker trips, and all further calls return with an error immediately, without the protected call being made at all. The breaker has three states: closed, in which calls pass through normally; open, in which calls fail fast without touching the struggling service; and half-open, in which a single trial call is allowed through to test whether the service has recovered. A successful trial closes the breaker. A failed trial opens it again.

The electrical analogy is exact enough to be useful. A circuit breaker in a building does not fix a short circuit. It stops the short circuit from starting a fire. A software circuit breaker does not fix a failing service. It stops the callers from turning a failing service into a dead one, and it stops the callers from tying up their own resources waiting on timeouts for calls that were never going to succeed. Fowler's account adds that not all errors should trip the breaker — some failures are normal, and belong to the workflow's ordinary logic — and that any change in breaker state should be logged and visible for monitoring.

For a workflow, the circuit breaker belongs in the plan as a named rule, not as an improvisation. The plan should say: this step calls that service; if the service fails this many times in this window, stop calling it, fail fast, and escalate. The threshold is a design decision, and it should be written down with its reasoning, because the threshold encodes a judgment about how much failure the service can absorb before the callers become part of the problem.

4. Cascades: how one failure becomes five

A single failing step is a problem. A single failing step that takes down the steps around it is a disaster, and the difference between the two is usually a design choice made long before the failure. Cascading failure is the name for it: one component's failure increases the load on the components around it, which fail in turn, until the whole system is down. The breaker exists to stop the cascade. But the cascade has other entry points, and a workflow plan should name them.

The first entry point is the parallel branch. The first article in this series described the fan-out pattern: one step splitting into several parallel branches that run at the same time, joining back together when all of them have finished. The join is where the cascade hides. If the join waits for all branches, then one failed branch holds the whole workflow hostage — every other branch's work is stranded behind a step that will never finish. If the join proceeds with a partial result, then the workflow continues with missing inputs, and the missing inputs become failures downstream. Neither behaviour is wrong in itself. What is wrong is a plan that does not say which one it uses. AWS Step Functions makes the choice explicit in its own way: a Distributed Map state fails with States.ExceedToleratedFailureThreshold when the number of failed items exceeds the threshold set in the state machine definition. The threshold is the plan's answer to the question "how many branch failures can this workflow absorb?" — and a plan without a threshold has no answer, which means the first branch failure decides the workflow's fate by default.

The second entry point is the shared dependency. Two steps that look independent may both depend on the same database, the same service, or the same human approver. When that dependency fails, both steps fail, and the workflow's failure count doubles for a single root cause. The diagnosis in section 1 helps here: two steps failing at the same time, with the same error, against the same dependency, is one failure, not two, and the response should be one response rather than two separate retry loops hammering the same struggling service.

The third entry point is the retry storm. A step fails, retries immediately, fails again, retries again — and meanwhile every other workflow in the system is doing the same thing against the same service, at the same moment. The retries synchronise, the load spikes, and the service that might have recovered in thirty seconds is buried under simultaneous retries. AWS Step Functions' retry model addresses this with its defaults: a retrier's MaxAttempts defaults to 3, its BackoffRate defaults to 2.0 — doubling the wait between attempts — and it supports jitter, randomising the wait so that simultaneous retries spread out instead of landing together.

The defence against cascades, in the end, is the same as the defence against every failure in this article: say it in the plan, before the failure. How many branch failures can the workflow absorb? Which dependencies are shared, and what happens when they fail? How many retries, with what backoff, with what jitter? A plan that answers those questions has bounded the blast radius of any single failure.

5. Escalation: who gets woken, and who decides

Every failure-handling design eventually reaches a question that no mechanism can answer: who decides now? The retry budget is spent. The breaker is open. The step is blocked, the heartbeat has stopped, and the plan's pre-written branches have run out. At that point the workflow needs a human — not as a failure of automation, but as its designed endpoint. The question is not whether to escalate. It is whom to escalate to, with what information, and with what authority.

The first part of that question is routing. An escalation that goes to the wrong person is an escalation that waits. The plan should name the escalation path for each step: who is responsible, how they are reached, and what happens if they do not respond. The monitoring article in this series examines what a monitor report should contain; the escalation version of the same question is what the report should contain when it is the thing that wakes someone up at two in the morning. The answer is the same in both cases: current step, progress, blockers — and, for a failure, the diagnosis from section 1, the heartbeat state from section 2, and the retry history. An escalation that says "step four failed" is a page that creates a second incident: the responder now has to investigate before they can act. An escalation that says "step four failed with a permanent input error, three retries spent, breaker open, upstream data missing since 01:40" is a page that lets the responder decide.

The second part is authority. The responder needs to know what they are allowed to decide: retry with new inputs, reroute around the failed step, compensate and abandon, or change the plan itself. Those are different levels of authority, and they belong to different people. A plan that does not say who can authorise compensation will either stall while someone finds the person who can, or proceed on an unauthorised decision that the audit will later have to untangle.

There is a temptation to design escalation out of the system — to build a workflow so automated that no human is ever needed. That temptation should be resisted. Business-rule failures are judgments. Permanent failures may require changing the plan. Timeouts with unknown outcomes require finding out what actually happened. None of those are automatable in the general case. The design this series keeps returning to is one in which the automation handles what it can, escalates what it cannot, and records everything either way. Tactile's catalogue reflects that shape: the monitor report observes, the step check settles, the audit reviews, and the human decides.

6. The rescue decision: retry, reroute, compensate, or abandon

Once the diagnosis is in and the escalation has landed, the responder faces a decision with four options, and the discipline is in choosing between them rather than defaulting to the first. The four are retry, reroute, compensate, and abandon — and each one is right for a different failure.

Retry is right for the transient failure, and only for the transient failure. The evidence is that the cause has passed: the service is responding again, the rate limit has reset, the network partition has healed. The retry should be bounded — AWS Step Functions' default of three attempts is a reasonable starting point — with backoff and jitter, and with the idempotency precondition the anatomy article establishes: running the step again must be safe even if the first attempt secretly succeeded.

Reroute is right when the step cannot succeed as written but the workflow can still reach its goal by another path. The primary supplier is down; the secondary supplier is available. The first laboratory is closed; the second can take the sample. Rerouting is the option that requires the most foresight, because the alternative path has to exist before the failure — a second supplier contracted, a second route tested, a fallback branch written into the plan. A workflow with no alternative path has no reroute option, whatever the responder wishes in the moment. The plan is where reroutes are born. The failure is where they are used.

Compensate is right when the workflow cannot go forward and must go back. Steps already completed have effects — a charge made, a reservation held, a treatment applied — and those effects must be undone, safely, in reverse order, before the workflow can be declared finished in failure rather than left half-done. The saga pattern, documented by Chris Richardson on microservices.io, is the field's canonical form of this idea: a saga is a reliably coordinated sequence of local transactions, and when a step fails, the changes made by the completed steps are undone by compensating transactions. The decision-level point is narrower: compensation is a designed capability, not an improvisation. Each step needs its compensating action written down before the workflow runs, because a step whose effects cannot be undone is a step the workflow cannot safely abandon.

Abandon is right when none of the other three applies, and it is the option that requires the most discipline to choose, because abandoning a workflow feels like admitting defeat. It is not. It is the recognition that the workflow's goal cannot be reached from here, that further attempts will consume budget and create risk without creating progress, and that the correct outcome is a clean stop: effects compensated where possible, the record updated, the stakeholders told, and the failure handed to the post-mortem. A workflow that cannot be abandoned is a workflow that will run forever, retrying a permanent failure until someone notices the bill.

The four options form a decision tree, and the tree should be written into the plan: if transient, retry within these bounds; else if an alternative path exists, reroute; else if the goal cannot be reached, compensate and abandon; else escalate. A responder with that tree can decide in minutes.

7. What cannot be undone

Compensation has limits, and the limits are worth stating plainly, because a workflow that assumes everything can be undone will discover otherwise at the worst possible moment. The saga pattern's own documentation names the two structural limits. First, there is no automatic rollback: unlike a database transaction, a saga's compensating transactions must be designed explicitly — a developer must write the undo for every step, and a step with no written undo has no way back. Second, there is no isolation: while a saga runs, other transactions can see its partial effects, and a compensating transaction cannot unsee what has already been seen. A customer who received a confirmation email cannot be un-notified. A sample that was consumed cannot be unconsumed. A message that was sent cannot be unsent.

The practical consequence is that a workflow plan must classify its steps by reversibility before it runs. Some steps are fully reversible: a reservation can be cancelled, a file can be deleted, a record can be corrected. Some steps are reversible with cost: a charge can be refunded, but the refund takes days and the customer has already been notified. Some steps are irreversible: a chemical mixed, a sample consumed, a message delivered. The plan should mark each step with its class, and the rescue decision in section 6 should be constrained by it. A workflow whose irreversible step has already run cannot be compensated back to its starting state. It can only be carried forward — completed by another route, or closed out with the irreversible effect acknowledged and recorded.

This is also where the distinction between a workflow and a database transaction matters most. A database can roll back. A workflow cannot, because a workflow's effects live in the world — in money moved, materials consumed, messages sent, people notified. The saga pattern exists precisely because the world does not have a rollback button, and its discipline is the discipline of designing the way back before going forward.

8. The post-mortem: how a failure becomes a lesson

A failure that is handled well and never written down is a failure that will happen again, to someone else, who will handle it from scratch. The post-mortem is the instrument that breaks that cycle, and the field's most developed account of it comes from Google's Site Reliability Engineering organisation, whose book on the subject states the principle plainly: a postmortem is a written record of an incident, its impact, the actions taken to mitigate or resolve it, the root cause or causes, and the follow-up actions to prevent it from recurring. The cost of failure, as the chapter's epigraph puts it, is education.

Google's postmortem culture has three features that a workflow's version should borrow. First, postmortems are triggered by defined criteria, set before the incident: user-visible downtime or degradation beyond a threshold, data loss of any kind, on-call intervention such as a rollback or reroute, a resolution time above a threshold, or a monitoring failure — which usually implies the incident was discovered by hand rather than by the monitor. The criteria matter because they remove the decision from the moment: nobody has to argue, in the aftermath, about whether this failure deserved a write-up.

Second, postmortems are blameless. Google's account is explicit: a blameless postmortem assumes that everyone involved had good intentions and did the right thing with the information they had, and focuses on the contributing causes without indicting any individual or team. The reason is practical, not sentimental. Blame culture teaches people to hide incidents, and hidden incidents are incidents that recur. The chapter traces the idea to healthcare and avionics, industries where mistakes can be fatal and where every mistake is treated as an opportunity to strengthen the system. You cannot fix people, but you can fix systems and processes to better support people making the right choices.

Third, postmortems are reviewed, shared, and stored. An unreviewed postmortem, Google's account says, might as well never have existed. The draft is assessed for completeness — was key incident data collected, was the root cause sufficiently deep, is the action plan appropriate — and then shared broadly and added to a repository of past incidents. A single postmortem teaches one team. A repository of postmortems teaches the organisation.

Tactile's catalogue contains the post-mortem's commercial form: the workflow_audit, $50 with a 48-hour turnaround, reviewing each step against its inputs, outputs, and checkpoints, with findings and recommendations. The audit is not a post-mortem — it reviews a workflow, not an incident — but it asks whether the plan's failure handling worked, and its findings feed the next version of the plan. A workflow that is audited after its failures is a workflow that learns. A workflow that is never audited is a workflow that will make the same ordering mistake twice, because its records were thrown away and its lessons were never written down.

Glossary

  • Transient failure: a failure caused by a passing condition — a network partition, a momentary overload — where retry is the right first move.
  • Permanent failure: a failure where the step cannot succeed as written, and retry will produce the same result.
  • Business-rule failure: a step that ran correctly and returned a refusal — a failed check, an exceeded limit — not a technical error.
  • Timeout: a step not finishing within the time allowed. A fact about the observer, not a diagnosis of the step.
  • Heartbeat: a periodic signal from a running step proving it is alive. A stopped heartbeat means dead or unreachable, not merely slow.
  • Circuit breaker: a pattern that stops calls to a failing service once failures reach a threshold, failing fast instead, with a trial call (half-open) to test recovery.
  • Cascading failure: one component's failure taking down the components around it, through shared dependencies, retry storms, or blocked joins.
  • Saga: a reliably coordinated sequence of local transactions, in which a failed step's predecessors are undone by compensating transactions.
  • Compensation: undoing the effects of completed steps, in reverse order, when a later step fails.
  • Idempotency: the property that running a step twice has the same effect as running it once — the precondition for safe retries.
  • Escalation: handing a failure to a human with the information and authority to decide, and recording the handover.
  • Post-mortem: a written record of an incident — impact, actions, root causes, follow-ups — written blamelessly so the failure becomes a lesson.
